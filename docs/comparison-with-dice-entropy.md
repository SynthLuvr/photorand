# PhotRand vs. Dice: A Comparison of Physical Entropy Sources

This document is a formal, evidence-grounded comparison of `photorand` — a
True Random Number Generator (TRNG) that harvests entropy from camera sensor
noise — against the canonical physical entropy source: rolling dice. Both are
*true* (non-deterministic, physical) sources rather than pseudo-random number
generators (PRNGs), which makes them natural to compare. The aim is an honest
assessment of where each is stronger, weaker, or simply different.

> **Scope note:** This is an analytical document, not a security audit of
> `photorand`. It assesses the *design as described in the source and docs of
> this repository*. Claims about the code are cited to the relevant files.

## 1. What "True Randomness" Means Here

Both dice and the camera sensor produce output that is not generated by an
algorithm, so neither is a PRNG. The distinction from a CSPRNG matters:

- A **TRNG** (dice, the photorand *seed*) samples a physical process. Its
  output is unpredictable even to an adversary who knows everything about the
  device, short of measuring the physical state at capture time.
- A **CSPRNG** (the photorand *engine*) is deterministic: given the same seed
  and state, it reproduces the same stream. Its security is *computational*.

A subtle but honest point: the underlying physics differ in kind.

- A **die** is governed by classical mechanics. In principle its trajectory is
  deterministic; it is only unpredictable because the outcome is exquisitely
  sensitive to initial conditions (spin, velocity, surface) that no one can
  track. This is *deterministic chaos* plus ignorance.
- The **sensor** harvests *shot noise* (quantum fluctuations in photon
  arrivals), *read noise* (electronic digitization noise), and *thermal noise*
  (dark current). Shot noise is quantum-mechanically indeterministic; the
  others are statistical-mechanical fluctuations.

For practical security this distinction is academic — both are unpredictable to
any classical adversary. But it is accurate to say the sensor's source rests on
physics that is "more fundamentally" indeterministic.

## 2. Entropy Rate and Throughput

### Dice

Information per roll is fixed by the die geometry:

| Die   | Outcomes | Entropy/roll (log₂) |
|-------|---------:|--------------------:|
| d6    |        6 |             2.585   |
| d20   |       20 |             4.322   |

Rolls required to reach common key strengths:

| Target   | d6 rolls (÷2.585) | d20 rolls (÷4.322) |
|----------|------------------:|-------------------:|
| 128 bits |                50 |                 30 |
| 256 bits |                99 |                 60 |
| 512 bits |               198 |                119 |

At a human pace of ~1–2 rolls/second, 256 bits takes roughly **1–2 minutes of
uninterrupted, carefully recorded rolling**.

The real-world dice workflow is **Diceware** (Reinhold): roll 5 dice to index
one word from a 7776-word list, giving **12.925 bits per word**. A 7-word
Diceware passphrase ≈ 90 bits; reaching 256 bits needs ~20 words, i.e. **100
dice rolls**. This is the honest benchmark for dice-based key generation.

### PhotRand

One RAW dark-frame capture yields far more. From the assessment table in
[`entropy-estimation.md`](entropy-estimation.md) (Sony A7-series, `DSC02111.ARW`,
FPN-reduced):

- **3.44 bits/symbol** (4-bit symbols), over a sampled grid giving
  **~20,573 bits** of measured min-entropy per frame.

However, the system is deliberately conservative about how much it *trusts*:
`estimate_entropy()` computes `max_seed_bytes = min(total_bits // 8, 64)` (see
`src/low_level/entropy.py`). So while ~20.5 kbits are harvested, only **64 bytes
(512 bits)** are emitted as the seed, with the remainder discarded. This is a
wide safety margin, not a claim that 20.5 kbits of seed are delivered.

**Net:** a single sub-second capture comfortably exceeds what ~200 d6 rolls
provide, and the design explicitly refuses to overstate the harvest.

## 3. Bias: Model and Handling

Bias is the central problem for any physical source, and the two handle it
very differently.

### Dice

A real die has a **fixed but unknown** manufacturing bias (uneven mass, rounded
edges). Mitigations are external and lossy:

- Assume fairness and accept the bias, or
- Apply the **von Neumann extractor** (compare pairs of rolls, output only on
  disagreement), which removes bias at the cost of discarding ~50–75% of rolls.

There is no inherent measurement of *how biased* a given die is; that would
require recording thousands of rolls and computing statistics yourself.

### PhotRand

The sensor's dominant bias is **fixed-pattern noise (FPN)** — dark-signal
non-uniformity and pixel-response non-uniformity that is a *deterministic,
per-sensor fingerprint*. If left in, it masquerades as entropy while being
predictable across captures.

`photorand` models this explicitly in `src/low_level/sample.py`:
`reduce_fixed_pattern_noise()` subtracts row-wise then column-wise medians
before extracting the 4 least-significant bits, leaving the stochastic floor.

The honesty of this step is striking and worth recording: as the project's own
table shows, the **raw (un-reduced) data looks *more* uniform** (χ² passes at
p≈0.99, 3.60 bits/sym) than the **FPN-reduced** data (χ² *fails* at p≈0.002,
3.44 bits/sym). The reason is that the deterministic fingerprint *spreads*
values, artificially inflating apparent uniformity. `photorand` deliberately
reports the lower, truer number and flags the χ² failure rather than hiding
behind the flattering raw result.

## 4. Conditioning and the "Whitening Trap"

A cryptographic hash is a **whitener, not an entropy multiplier**: a 512-bit
digest of a 50-bit source still holds ~50 bits. Hashed output *passes
statistical tests regardless* of the input's true entropy, which is why
test-passing on conditioned output is not evidence of randomness.

- **Dice** are typically used raw. If one were to hash dice rolls, the digest
  would look perfectly uniform even for a loaded die — masking the problem
  rather than revealing it.
- **PhotRand** whitens with **SHA3-512** (`src/low_level/hash.py`) but, per the
  NIST SP 800-90B "measure first, then condition" principle, **estimates
  min-entropy on the raw pool *before* hashing** (`src/low_level/entropy.py`).
  This is the project's principal methodological advantage over ad-hoc physical
  sources, dice included.

The single most transferable lesson of this comparison: **measure the raw
source before you condition it.** A dice workflow that recorded rolls and
computed min-entropy would be meaningfully stronger than the typical "just roll
a d20" approach — and that discipline is exactly what `photorand` bakes in.

## 5. Measurement and Health Checking

### Dice

None built-in. The user bears the full burden of fairness testing, which in
practice is almost never done rigorously for casual use.

### PhotRand

`estimate_entropy()` runs a battery of NIST SP 800-90B estimators and tests,
surfacing a single `overall_status` of `GOOD` / `WARN` / `LOW` / `FAIL`:

- **Most Common Value (MCV)** min-entropy, §6.1 — the primary, most conservative
  bound.
- **Collision** min-entropy, §6.3.
- **Shannon** entropy (an upper bound).
- **Chi-square** uniformity test (α = 0.01).
- **Repetition Count Test**, §4.4.1 — detects stuck-at faults.
- **Adaptive Proportion Test**, §4.4.2 — detects distributional drift.

Conservative min-entropy is `min(MCV, collision)`, and a `FAIL` (a tripped
health check) means "do not use this seed." Failures are **self-reporting**;
dice failures are silent.

> **Honest caveat — collision estimator.** `photorand`'s collision estimator is
> a **non-standard adaptation** of §6.3: it records the distance between
> *consecutive occurrences of each symbol value* (per-value inter-arrival),
> rather than the NIST procedure of scanning and resetting on each collision.
> The code's own docstring is transparent about this. The MCV estimator and the
> two health tests (§4.4.1/§4.4.2) appear faithful to the standard. For
> high-assurance use, the collision variant should be validated against the NIST
> reference implementation; until then, treat the reported min-entropy as a
> conservative *estimate*, not a certified bound.

## 6. Expansion (CSPRNG)

### Dice

No expansion layer: you harvest exactly as many bits as you roll.

### PhotRand

A single 64-byte seed drives a **ChaCha20** CSPRNG (`src/low_level/csprng.py`),
keyed with seed bytes `[:32]` (256-bit key) and `[32:48]` (128-bit nonce),
optionally salted with a nanosecond timestamp and PID. The keystream is produced
by encrypting zeros, yielding effectively unlimited high-speed output from one
frame.

> **Honest caveat — what is and isn't "true".** Only the **512-bit seed** is
> physically true. Everything the engine emits *beyond* 512 bits is ChaCha20
> output — computationally secure pseudo-randomness, not true randomness. This
> is the correct and standard design, but it should not be described as
> delivering unbounded *true* randomness. The salting (time + PID) defends
> against re-use across runs, not against an adversary.

## 7. Trust Model and Auditability

This is where the two diverge most sharply, and where **dice are stronger**.

| Source | Trust surface |
|--------|---------------|
| Dice   | The die is fair; the roll is honest; the surface is level. All directly **observable by a human**. |
| Sensor | Camera firmware is not adversarially post-processing RAW data; the sensor is uncompromised; `rawpy` faithfully exposes the raw grid; the host OS/process is not tampering. Largely **opaque**. |

`photorand`'s health checks (§4.4) mitigate *statistical* anomalies — drift,
stuck values, gross bias — but they cannot detect a *clever* adversary who
controls firmware and supplies a stream that is statistically perfect yet
predictable to them. For the highest-assurance offline key generation,
dice (or coins) offer a simpler, more auditable trust story: a human can witness
the entire randomness event.

A further honest note on the source: `rawpy` reads the camera's embedded RAW,
which some manufacturers compress or black-level-shift. "RAW" is not always
truly raw, and a non-dark-frame capture (signal dominating noise) yields far
less entropy — surfaced as a `LOW` status, but worth stating.

## 8. Failure Modes

- **Dice:** loaded or unbalanced die, biased rolling technique, mis-recording.
  Failures are usually *visible*.
- **PhotRand:** non-dark-frame capture (entropy drops → `LOW`); a tripped health
  check (`FAIL`); an insufficient or compromised sensor; adversarial firmware.
  Statistical failures are *self-reported*; adversarial ones are not.

## 9. At a Glance

| Dimension              | Dice                                   | PhotRand |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------|----------|
| Physical phenomenon    | Classical chaos (deterministic in principle) | Quantum + thermal noise |
| Entropy / symbol       | d6: 2.585; d20: 4.322 bits             | ~3.44 bits (4-bit symbols) |
| Throughput             | ~100 rolls for 256 bits                | ~20.5 kbits per frame (512-bit seed emitted) |
| Bias model             | Fixed, unknown                         | Known structure (FPN), explicitly removed |
| Conditioning           | None / ad-hoc                          | SHA3-512 whitening *after* measuring |
| Entropy measurement    | None (user must DIY)                   | NIST SP 800-90B MCV/collision/χ²/health |
| Expansion              | None                                   | ChaCha20 CSPRNG (seed-limited true, rest pseudo) |
| Trust surface          | Minimal, observable                    | Large, opaque (firmware/sensor/OS) |
| Auditability           | Trivial, witnessed                     | Relies on assessment |
| Cost / setup           | Cheap, pocketable, offline             | Camera + computer + dependencies |

## 10. Conclusion

Dice and `photorand` are **complementary**, occupying opposite ends of a
trade-off rather than competing on a single axis:

- **Dice** win on **auditability and minimal trust**: ideal for one-off,
  high-assurance, offline key material a human wants to witness, at the cost of
  being slow and statistically unmeasured in typical use.
- **PhotRand** wins on **throughput, measured entropy, and built-in health
  checking**: ideal for generating large volumes (keys, passwords, seeds)
  quickly, at the cost of a larger and less transparent trust surface.

The most important takeaway is methodological. `photorand` formalizes a
discipline that dice users usually skip: **measure the raw source before
conditioning it**, and refuse to confuse a passing statistical test on
whitened output with evidence of entropy. A dice-based workflow that adopted
the same NIST SP 800-90B assessment would be substantially stronger than the
common "roll and trust" practice — and closing exactly that gap is the purpose
`photorand` is built to serve for its sensor-based source.

**Practical guidance:**

1. For routine, high-volume generation, `photorand` with a **dark-frame
   capture** and a passing assessment is appropriate and convenient.
2. For the highest-assurance one-off keys where a human must be able to attest
   to the randomness event, prefer or cross-check with dice/coins, given
   `photorand`'s larger trust surface.
3. Treat the collision-estimator variant (§5 caveat) as a conservative
   estimate, not a certified bound, until validated against the reference.
4. Remember only the 512-bit seed is *true* randomness; the ChaCha20 engine's
   expanded stream is computationally secure, not physically true.

## Appendix A — Derivation of Dice Figures

Per-roll entropy is the base-2 logarithm of the number of outcomes:

- d6:  log₂(6)  = 2.585 bits
- d20: log₂(20) = 4.322 bits

Rolls to reach a target `B` bits ≈ `ceil(B / bits-per-roll)`. Diceware gives
log₂(6⁵) = log₂(7776) = 12.925 bits per 5-roll word, so 256 bits requires
`ceil(256 / 12.925) ≈ 20` words = **100 rolls**, consistent with the per-roll
figures above.
